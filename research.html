The escalation

LLMs are advancing fast — and attackers are advancing with them. The same models that write code and reason about systems can be turned to generate novel exploits, rewrite malware on the fly, and plan attacks autonomously. The result is a threat that no longer runs a fixed payload.

Tomorrow's malware will learn from every attempt, mutate its own code to slip past detection, and adapt to new defenses on its own. Early demonstrations have already shown the concept — researchers have built proof-of-concept agents with persistent memory and LLM-driven code rewriting that can learn from failed attempts and mutate their payloads between runs. If attackers gain this, our defenses cannot stay still.

Why the stakes are higher now

Factor 01

We are handing AI the keys

To make AI useful, we give it deep access to our data, our code, and our infrastructure. That same access is exactly what an adaptive attacker inherits the moment it turns one of those systems against us.

Factor 02

The knowledge asymmetry is built in

Advanced models are trained on more data about our systems and behavior than any defender can hold in view. An adversary that inherits that knowledge can target weaknesses the defender hasn't mapped yet.

More capability, more access, and more knowledge of us — a bigger blast radius than any malware before it.

The approach

You cannot train a defense against an attack it has never been allowed to face. To defend against an attacker that learns, our defensive systems have to be trained against one.

That means building a controlled, sandboxed adversary — safely mimicking the self-mutating, LLM-powered malware described above, with no path to any real target — and using it to stress-test and harden the AI systems that defend us. It is the same principle behind every robust system: you strengthen it by exposing it, under control, to the real threat. The safety of that simulation is itself a core research problem, and it is the heart of what I want to study.

What I want to learn — three areas

I

Safely simulating self-learning attacks

Adversarial simulation · Sandboxed testbeds
Question

How do we mimic self-mutating, LLM-powered malware realistically enough to train a defense — while guaranteeing the simulation can never escape its sandbox?

The gap

Realistic adaptive-attack testbeds barely exist, and the ones that do carry real risk. A safe, reproducible adversary is the missing raw material for training defenses.

What I want to learn

How to build controlled testbeds that reproduce a learning attacker's behavior under strict containment — network whitelisting, isolated execution, and an immutable audit log — so the simulation is realistic and safely contained.

Why it matters

Every defense in the following directions needs a realistic adversary to be trained and measured against.

II

Training defensive AI to withstand adaptive attacks

Adversarial training · Blue-team AI
Question

Can our LLM-based security systems be trained against a learning adversary so they adapt as fast as the threat does — and harden what they protect without a human in the critical path?

The gap

Most defenses are static; they are updated after an attack succeeds, not before. A defender that keeps pace with a mutating attacker is still largely unsolved.

What I want to learn

How adversarial training works in practice — using simulated attackers to continuously test and harden defensive models, with the discipline to ensure automated responses never cause more harm than they prevent.

Why it matters

Defenders are perpetually outpaced. Training against the real threat is one of the few ways to close the gap.

III

Safe autonomy under deep access

AI safety · Self-modification · Least privilege
Question

We give AI systems deep access to our infrastructure so they can be useful. But that same access becomes a liability if the system is compromised or behaves unexpectedly. How do we let AI act autonomously while ensuring it cannot exceed its intended scope — even when it modifies its own behavior?

The gap

Self-modifying AI with broad privileges is being deployed faster than we can verify it stays safe. Today's guardrails are ad hoc rules, not provable constraints.

What I want to learn

How least-privilege architectures and validation-before-execution keep autonomous systems safe — understanding constraints that can be formally checked, so a system's safety guarantees hold even as it updates itself.

Why it matters

The access and knowledge we grant AI is what makes an attack on it so dangerous. Containing that risk is foundational AI safety.

A note on responsibility

Simulating attacks has one purpose: to train the defense. Every experiment in this direction must stay inside an isolated sandbox, with no path to a real target, and every action logged for accountability. Offensive knowledge carries an obligation — to contain it, to disclose responsibly, and to build the defense alongside the demonstration. That discipline is not a limit on the research; it is the research.
