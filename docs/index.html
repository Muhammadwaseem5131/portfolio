How I got here

About

I bricked the family router at sixteen and spent a week learning why.

The router came back eventually. What stayed was the habit of taking a thing apart until I understood the failure rather than just the fix. Two years on I run a home lab out of my bedroom — pfSense at the edge, a three-host Windows domain, and a Kali box I use to attack my own network so I know what the logs look like from both sides of an intrusion.

The lab produces far more log data than I can read, which is how I ended up in machine learning. Writing detection rules only catches what you already thought of; a model that has learned what normal looks like can flag the thing you did not. That gap is the work I want to do — and it is why I am applying to Korea, where security research and applied AI sit in the same departments rather than in separate buildings.

Lab 01 · 2025

Teaching a model what my network normally does

Isolation Forest over Zeek connection logs from a three-host lab.

Question
Could an unsupervised model flag beaconing traffic I had never labelled for it?
Method
Two weeks of Zeek conn logs, engineered features for interval regularity and payload-size variance, then fit an Isolation Forest and ran a real beacon through it.
Result
It caught the beacon — and flagged my own nightly backup job as well. Tuning out that false positive taught me more than the detection did: base rates matter more than model choice.

Lab 02 · 2025

Walking my own domain the way an attacker would

Active Directory attack path, then the detections that should have caught it.

Question
Starting from one ordinary domain user, how far could I get before anything in the lab logged it?
Method
Collected the graph with BloodHound, Kerberoasted a service account with a weak password, and moved to the file server. Then replayed the identical path with Sysmon and Wazuh recording everything.
Result
Four steps to Domain Admin; only two of them produced an alert under the default configuration. I wrote the Sigma rules for the other two, which is the part of the exercise that was actually worth keeping.

Lab 03 · 2026

Rebuilding an intrusion from what the SIEM kept

Timeline reconstruction against my own scripted intrusion, a week cold.

Question
Could I reconstruct the timeline of an intrusion using only what my logging pipeline had retained — no memory of running it?
Method
Ran a scripted intrusion against the lab, left it a week, then worked purely from Wazuh alerts, Sysmon event IDs and Zeek logs to write the incident timeline.
Result
Initial access and persistence came back cleanly. Lateral movement was invisible — Sysmon Event ID 3 was never being forwarded. Fixing the pipeline mattered more than any rule I could have written on top of it.

Lab 04 · 2026

A phishing URL classifier, and how accuracy lied to me

Lexical features only, evaluated at a realistic base rate rather than a balanced one.

Question
How much of a phishing URL is detectable from its text alone, with no page fetch and no reputation feed?
Method
Built lexical features — length, character entropy, subdomain depth, token counts, TLD — over a public URL corpus, then compared logistic regression against gradient boosting on precision-recall rather than accuracy.
Result
0.96 accuracy on a balanced split, which is meaningless: re-weighted to the prevalence a real mail gateway sees, precision collapsed. The model was fine. The evaluation was the finding.

Where I want to take this

Open questions

Every lab below left me with a question I could not answer with a bedroom network and no supervision. These are the three I want to work on.

01

Detection that survives a realistic base rate

My phishing classifier looked strong until I evaluated it at the rate real traffic actually carries, and my anomaly model flagged my own backup job. Benchmarks reward balanced data; networks are not balanced.

Need
Real traffic at volume, and a lab that treats false-positive cost as a first-class metric.

02

Can a detection model be evaded on purpose?

I built models that catch an attack I wrote myself. I have no idea how they hold up against someone shaping traffic specifically to slip past them — which is the only case that matters in production.

Need
Adversarial machine learning taught properly, and people to test my assumptions against.

03

What evidence actually survives an incident

Reconstructing my own intrusion a week later, the limit was never my analysis — it was what the pipeline had kept. Deciding what to retain before you need it is a design problem, not a tooling one.

Need
Systems bigger than one person's lab, where retention has a real cost.

Why Korea, and what happens after

Study plan

I am applying to the Department of AI Cyber Security (인공지능사이버보안학과) at Korea University Sejong and the Department of Smart Security (스마트보안학과) at Konyang University. Both teach detection and machine learning inside one department rather than treating security as a networking elective and AI as a maths one — which is the exact seam my own lab work keeps falling into. Korea University Sejong is the closer fit for the anomaly-detection research I want to do; Konyang's programme is the more applied of the two, and its industry placement is the part I would use hardest.

Pakistan has a growing number of organisations running enough infrastructure to need real detection engineering, and very few people trained to build it. After the degree I want to work in a SOC long enough to learn what detection actually costs at scale, then take that back and teach it — a detection-engineering curriculum in Urdu is not something that currently exists, and I am not convinced it should stay that way.

  3. 2032 onward

    Three to four years building detections somewhere they are under genuine load, then back to Pakistan to teach the same thing in Urdu. The gap at home is not talent — it is that nobody has written the course.
